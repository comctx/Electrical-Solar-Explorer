Beginner Start Here

  1. These diagrams show electrical relationships, not exact cable routing or installation instructions.
  2. Branch-circuit devices are normally connected in parallel, so each load receives the intended circuit voltage.
  3. A switch is placed in the control path to a load such as a light; the neutral generally continues to the load rather than being used as the normal switch control conductor.
  4. The equipment-grounding path is for fault conditions and is not the normal load-current path.
  5. Breaker rating, conductor size, box fill, receptacle count, required circuits, GFCI/AFCI rules, and cable type must follow the equipment instructions and electrical code that applies to the real installation.

Basic 120 V Receptacle Circuit

The branch breaker supplies the ungrounded conductor. The neutral provides the normal return path. The equipment-grounding conductor bonds exposed metal and provides an intended fault-current path.

Single-Pole Light Switch Circuit

Conceptually, the switch controls the ungrounded path to the light. The neutral continues to the light. Actual box/cable arrangements vary.

Multiple Receptacles on One Branch Circuit

The receptacles are electrically in parallel. Each one is connected across the branch hot and neutral—not in series through the appliances.

Light and Receptacle on the Same Branch — Concept

A branch circuit can serve more than one type of load when permitted by the design and applicable rules. The receptacle remains unswitched while the light is controlled by its switch.

Kitchen Small-Appliance / GFCI Concept

Kitchen countertop receptacles often require dedicated small-appliance branch circuits and GFCI protection, with AFCI and other requirements depending on the current code and location. This drawing shows the protection relationship only.

Bathroom Receptacle / GFCI Concept

Bathroom receptacles have dedicated protection and branch-circuit requirements. This diagram shows the functional relationship, not the permitted circuit-sharing rules for any particular jurisdiction.

Laundry Circuit Concept

A washer and dryer may involve separate branch circuits. A typical electric dryer is a 120/240 V load that may require two ungrounded conductors, neutral, and equipment ground depending on the appliance.

240 V Appliance Circuit Concept

A 240 V load uses two ungrounded conductors from opposite legs. Some appliances also require a neutral for 120 V internal loads; some do not. Equipment grounding remains separate from normal load current.

Overload Lessons to Remember

Loads Add Together

On one branch circuit, simultaneous loads add. Several moderate loads can exceed the circuit's intended capacity even when each device is normal by itself.

Large Loads Often Need Dedicated Circuits

Heating equipment, cooking appliances, laundry equipment, HVAC, and other large loads commonly have dedicated circuit requirements.

Never Upsize a Breaker Just to Stop Trips

The breaker protects the circuit conductors and equipment. A larger breaker can create a fire hazard if the wiring and equipment were not designed for it.

Continuous Loads Have Extra Rules

Some loads that operate for long periods are subject to additional code loading rules. The exact requirement depends on the application and code.

Motor / Compressor Starting Current

Some equipment draws higher current while starting. Circuit design and protection account for this differently from simple steady resistive loads.

Use Dedicated Kitchen / Bath / Laundry Rules

Residential electrical codes contain special branch-circuit and protection requirements for these areas. Do not treat them like unrestricted general-use outlets.

Safety Boundary

Educational diagrams only. They intentionally omit exact conductor sizes, breaker selections, cable types, terminal methods, box-fill calculations, required receptacle spacing, service calculations, and jurisdiction-specific rules. Fixed residential wiring can cause lethal shock, arc flash, and fire. Real work must follow the applicable electrical code, permits/inspection requirements, manufacturer instructions, and qualified-person practices. Never rely on conductor color alone.
