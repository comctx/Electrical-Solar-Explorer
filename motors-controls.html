Basic — Start Here

An electric motor converts electrical energy into mechanical rotation. First learn the motor itself. Then Technical and Advanced levels add the control equipment, terminal names, and diagnostic concepts around it.

Stator

The stationary magnetic part of the motor. In many AC motors, windings in the stator create a rotating magnetic field.

Stationary
Rotor

The rotating part inside the stator. Electromagnetic forces produce torque on the rotor.

Rotating
Shaft

Carries torque from the rotor to the machine, fan, pump, gearbox, pulley, or other load.

Mechanical output
Bearings

Support the shaft and let it rotate with low friction. Bearing wear is a common source of motor noise and vibration.

Support
Windings

Insulated coils of wire that create magnetic fields when current flows.

Electromagnetic
Cooling fan

Moves air over the motor frame or through the motor to remove heat.

Temperature control

Motor Types

Single-Phase Induction

Common in homes and small equipment. Many use a start winding and capacitor to create starting torque.

Three-Phase Induction

Widely used in industry. Three-phase power naturally creates a rotating magnetic field.

DC Brushed Motor

Uses brushes and a commutator to switch current in the rotating armature.

Brushless DC (BLDC)

Uses electronic commutation instead of brushes. Common in fans, tools, vehicles, and automation.

Synchronous Motor

Rotor speed locks to the rotating magnetic field rather than operating with induction-motor slip.

Stepper / Servo

Used when position and motion control are important. Servos normally include feedback; steppers move in discrete steps.

Motor Starting Methods

Different starters all have the same basic job — get the motor from stopped to running — but they do it in different ways.

DOL / Direct-on-Line Starter

Connects a motor directly to the supply through a contactor and overload relay. It is simple and common, but starting current and starting torque are relatively high.

Full-voltage start
Star-Delta / Wye-Delta Starter

Starts a suitable six-lead three-phase motor in star (wye) to reduce starting current and torque, then reconnects it in delta for normal running.

Reduced-voltage start
Soft Starter

Uses power electronics to gradually increase motor voltage during starting, reducing electrical and mechanical shock.

Electronic ramp
VFD

Controls both voltage and frequency, allowing controlled acceleration plus speed control while the motor is running.

Start + speed control
Think of it this way: DOL = full voltage immediately; star-delta = temporary reduced-voltage connection; soft starter = electronic voltage ramp; VFD = controlled frequency and voltage.

Associated Motor Parts & Controls Technical

  • Start capacitor: provides phase shift and extra starting torque for certain single-phase motors.
  • Run capacitor: remains in the circuit while running on capacitor-run designs to improve operating characteristics.
  • Centrifugal switch: mechanically disconnects a start winding or start capacitor after the motor accelerates on some designs.
  • Potential/current relay: can switch start components based on motor electrical conditions.
  • Contactor: electrically operated power switch used to connect and disconnect a motor.
  • Overload relay: protects against sustained motor overcurrent/overheating; it is different from short-circuit protection.
  • Motor starter: commonly combines a contactor with overload protection and control functions.
  • Fuse/circuit breaker: provides branch-circuit and short-circuit protection as designed for the system.
  • VFD: variable-frequency drive changes AC frequency and voltage to control compatible AC motor speed and torque.
  • Soft starter: reduces electrical and mechanical stress during starting without providing full variable-speed control like a VFD.
  • Encoder: measures shaft position, speed, or direction for feedback systems.
  • Thermal protector: opens or signals when motor temperature exceeds a safe limit.
  • Brushes: sliding electrical contacts used in brushed motors.
  • Commutator: segmented rotating switch used with brushes in DC motors and universal motors.
  • Coupling: connects the motor shaft to another shaft while accommodating the intended alignment/motion.
  • Gearbox: trades speed for torque or changes the mechanical relationship between motor and load.

DOL (Direct-on-Line) Starter Terminal Connections Technical

A DOL starter applies full line voltage to the motor when the contactor closes. This section shows where the major conductors terminate in a typical three-phase DOL motor-starter concept. Terminal names vary by manufacturer, so treat this as a relationship diagram rather than a field-wiring instruction.

Technical view: follow the three power conductors through the contactor and overload relay to the motor. The contactor coil is a separate control circuit using terminals commonly labeled A1 and A2.

L1 / L2 / L3

Common labels for the contactor's incoming line-side power terminals.

T1 / T2 / T3

Common labels for the contactor's outgoing load-side power terminals toward the overload relay and motor.

A1 / A2

Common coil-terminal labels. Applying the correct control voltage across the coil causes the contactor to change state.

95 / 96

A common overload-relay normally closed auxiliary contact designation used in a control circuit so an overload trip can release the contactor.

Motor U / V / W or T1 / T2 / T3

Motor terminal markings vary. Use the motor nameplate and connection diagram for the actual lead arrangement.

PE / Frame

The protective equipment-grounding connection bonds exposed metal. It is separate from the normal phase-current paths.

Advanced view: a reversing starter uses two contactors. Reversing the order of two phases changes the rotating-field sequence and reverses a compatible three-phase motor. Electrical and mechanical interlocking are used so the forward and reverse contactors cannot close together.

Phase sequence

Changing the order of two phase conductors reverses the rotating magnetic field and therefore reverses a compatible three-phase motor.

Interlocking

Forward and reverse contactors are prevented from closing together because that could create a severe fault condition.

Diagnostic thinking

If a motor will not start, separate the problem into source power, control power, coil command, contactor state, overload state, motor leads, and mechanical load.

Measurement points

Professionals distinguish line-side voltage, load-side voltage, coil voltage, phase current, insulation condition, and mechanical symptoms rather than replacing components at random.

Safety boundary: industrial motor circuits can involve hazardous mains voltage, high fault current, stored energy, rotating machinery, and automatic restart. This diagram is educational only. Real installation and troubleshooting require the correct drawings, equipment instructions, lockout/tagout practices, applicable code, and qualified-person procedures.

Star-Delta / Wye-Delta Starting Technical

A star-delta starter uses three contactors to change the motor winding connection during acceleration. The motor must be designed and terminalled for this starting method.

Technical view: the main contactor connects the supply, the star contactor temporarily joins the three winding ends together for starting, and the delta contactor reconnects the windings for normal running. Star and delta contactors must never be closed at the same time.

Main contactor

Connects the three-phase source to the motor starter power circuit.

Star / Wye contactor

Temporarily joins U2, V2, and W2 together during the reduced-voltage starting period.

Delta contactor

Reconnects the motor windings into the required delta arrangement for normal running.

Timer / controller

Determines when the starter changes from star to delta after the motor has accelerated.

Interlocking

Mechanical and electrical interlocks prevent the star and delta contactors from closing together.

Six motor leads

The starter needs access to both ends of all three stator windings. The motor nameplate and manufacturer diagram determine the actual terminal arrangement.

Advanced view: star starting reduces each winding's voltage compared with delta operation. As a result, line starting current is reduced, but starting torque is also reduced substantially. The method is suitable only where the motor and load can accelerate with that reduced torque.

Reduced starting current

For the classic star-delta method on a motor intended to run in delta at the line voltage, starting line current is roughly one-third of the corresponding DOL starting current under idealized conditions.

Reduced starting torque

Motor torque is approximately proportional to the square of applied winding voltage, so star starting provides roughly one-third of the DOL starting torque under the same idealized assumptions.

Open transition

Many starters briefly disconnect the motor between star and delta. The transition can create current and torque transients depending on speed, phase relationship, and load.

Closed transition

More complex systems can use transition components to avoid a completely open interval, reducing some transition disturbances.

Transition timing

Changing too early can produce a large current surge because the motor has not accelerated enough. Changing too late can leave the motor operating unnecessarily in the reduced-torque star condition.

Motor suitability

The motor must have the correct winding voltage rating and six accessible winding terminals. Not every three-phase motor is suitable for star-delta starting on a given supply.

Safety boundary: star-delta power wiring is not a field-wiring recipe. Incorrect contactor sequencing or winding connections can create a severe phase-to-phase fault or damage the motor. Use the actual starter schematic, motor nameplate, manufacturer documentation, lockout/tagout, and qualified-person procedures.

Advanced Motor-Control Troubleshooting Advanced

A good diagnostic sequence follows the energy and control paths rather than guessing at parts.

Motor dead, contactor not pulled in

Think first about control power, STOP/START logic, overload auxiliary state, permissives/interlocks, coil voltage, and coil condition.

Contactor pulled in, motor dead

Separate line-side power from load-side power. Consider open protection, failed contacts, overload power path, open motor leads, and internal motor condition.

Motor runs backward

On a compatible three-phase motor, rotation relates to phase sequence. Verify the intended machine direction and documentation before any corrective work.

Overload trips repeatedly

Consider actual mechanical load, phase-current imbalance, supply condition, acceleration time, motor connection, cooling, ambient conditions, and overload settings rather than simply resetting it repeatedly.

One phase missing

Single-phasing can cause abnormal current, poor torque, overheating, and failure. Compare all phases rather than assuming that seeing voltage at one point proves the whole power path is healthy.

VFD-driven motor

Separate incoming supply issues from drive faults, parameter/configuration issues, output-side problems, motor problems, and mechanical-load problems.
