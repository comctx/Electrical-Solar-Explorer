Beginner Start Here

A thyristor is a semiconductor switch built for controlling power. The three names you will see most often here are SCR, TRIAC, and DIAC.

SCR

A silicon-controlled rectifier is a one-direction latching switch. A gate pulse turns it on, and current through the main path keeps it on until that current falls low enough.

DC / controlled rectification
TRIAC

A TRIAC is a bidirectional thyristor used mainly with AC. It can control current on both halves of an AC waveform.

AC power control
DIAC

A DIAC is a two-terminal bidirectional trigger device. It is often paired with a TRIAC to help produce a repeatable trigger point.

Trigger device
Why they are useful

They can control heaters, lamps, motor soft starters, controlled rectifiers, and other power loads without mechanical contacts.

High-power switching
Latching

An SCR or TRIAC does not behave like an ordinary transistor switch. After triggering, main current usually determines when it turns off.

Important behavior
Ratings matter

Voltage, current, surge current, holding current, gate trigger requirements, switching rate, and temperature all matter in real equipment.

Check the datasheet
In Other Words
SCR: trigger it, then current keeps it latched on.
TRIAC: an AC switch that can conduct in either direction.
DIAC: a small trigger device often used to fire a TRIAC.

Selection & Troubleshooting Notes Advanced

Check the exact trigger requirement

Gate trigger current and voltage vary by part and temperature. A controller must provide the required trigger under worst-case conditions.

Watch heat

On-state voltage drop multiplied by load current produces heat. High-current applications often require serious thermal design.

Expect leakage

Solid-state devices and modules can pass small off-state currents. “Off” is not the same as mechanically isolated.

Consider the load

Resistive, inductive, capacitive, transformer, motor, and electronic loads can stress the switch very differently.

Verify turn-off conditions

If current never falls below holding current, a thyristor may remain latched even after the gate signal is removed.

Use the datasheet

Voltage, repetitive peak ratings, RMS/current ratings, surge limits, thermal impedance, gate limits, and package pinout are device-specific.

Where Thyristors Are Used

Heater control

SCR and TRIAC controllers regulate power to resistive heaters using burst firing or phase control.

Lighting dimmers

TRIAC phase control has long been used with compatible AC lighting loads.

Motor soft starters

Back-to-back SCRs can gradually increase applied AC voltage during motor starting.

Controlled rectifiers

SCR bridges can control the average DC output from an AC source.

Crowbar protection

An SCR can intentionally short a protected rail after an overvoltage trigger so an upstream protective device clears the fault.

Industrial power control

High-power thyristor assemblies are used where rugged high-voltage and high-current switching is required.

Safety

Many thyristor applications involve hazardous mains or industrial voltages. This page teaches device behavior and terminal relationships, not live-work procedures. De-energize equipment, verify absence of voltage, discharge stored energy, use proper overcurrent protection, and follow the equipment manufacturer and applicable electrical-safety practices.
