What Is a Schematic?

A schematic is a simplified drawing that shows how electrical parts are connected and how the circuit is intended to work. It is about electrical relationships, not the physical location or shape of the real parts.

Symbols = Components

A lamp, switch, resistor, relay coil, motor, and battery each have a standardized-style symbol.

Lines = Conductors

Lines show electrical connections. A junction dot usually means conductors are connected at that point.

Labels = Clues

Reference names, terminal numbers, voltage labels, and wire identifiers help you follow the circuit.

You do not need to memorize everything at once. Learn the common symbols first and recognize their job in the circuit.

The 7-Step Reading Method

  1. Find the power source. Locate the battery, DC supply, transformer secondary, or AC source.
  2. Identify the return path. Find the negative, neutral, common, or other return reference used by that schematic.
  3. Locate protection. Look for fuses, circuit breakers, overloads, and other protective devices.
  4. Find the control device. Switches, relay contacts, pushbuttons, sensors, and transistor switches decide when current can flow.
  5. Find the load. Lamps, motors, heaters, relay coils, solenoids, and electronic circuits use the electrical energy.
  6. Trace one path at a time. Follow from source → protection → control → load → return. Do not try to understand the whole drawing at once.
  7. Check labels and states. NO/NC contacts are normally shown in their de-energized or normal state unless the drawing says otherwise.

Follow a Simple Series Circuit

Press the button to see the current path. With the switch closed, there is one continuous route from the source through the fuse and lamp and back to the source.

Current has one continuous path. In a series circuit, the same current flows through every series component.

Series vs. Parallel

Series

Components are arranged one after another in the same path. The same current flows through each series component. Opening one point stops the entire path.

Parallel

Loads connect across the same two nodes. Each active branch has the branch voltage across it, and the branch currents add together at the source.

Reading trick: If the drawing splits into two or more paths and later rejoins, you are looking at parallel branches.

Junctions, Crossings, and Nodes

Junction Dot

When conductors meet with a solid dot, that dot represents a common electrical node.

Crossing Lines

Do not assume crossing lines are connected. Use the drawing's junction-dot convention and legend.

Important: Schematic conventions can vary by drawing standard and manufacturer. Always check the drawing legend, notes, and device documentation.

Relay Schematics: Coil and Contacts

A relay can appear in more than one place on a schematic. The coil and its contacts may be separated on the page but tied together by the same reference label.

Relay Coil K1

When the coil is energized, the relay changes the state of contacts labeled K1 elsewhere in the drawing.

NO / NC Contacts

Normally Open and Normally Closed describe the contact state in its normal, de-energized condition unless the drawing notes otherwise.

Try This Reading Exercise

A 12 V source feeds a fuse, then a closed switch, then a lamp, then returns to the source. Where would you first look if the lamp is dark but the source measures 12 V?

Practice in Circuit Lab
Trace the path one section at a time: check both sides of the fuse, then both sides of the switch, then the lamp connections and return path. The first point where the expected voltage disappears helps locate the open or faulty section.

Safety Note

Use schematics to understand circuit operation, not as permission to work on energized equipment. Real equipment may contain hazardous voltage, stored energy, multiple sources, interlocks, and manufacturer-specific requirements. Follow applicable procedures, manufacturer instructions, and qualified-person requirements.
